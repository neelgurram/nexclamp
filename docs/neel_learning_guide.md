# Neel's learning guide

*Drafted 2026-09-13 for the handoff's "Neel's required knowledge" list. Before the frozen
study, you should be able to explain all 14 items without help.*

**How to use this guide.**

- There is one item per section and one idea at a time.
- Each section follows the same order: an everyday analogy, the idea in short steps, the
  precise definition, a small worked example, and two questions to test yourself.

Every number carries a label so you know how much to trust it:

- **[File fact]**: read directly from a model or config file in this repository.
- **[Upstream data]**: reference data from the model's own repository, generated by its
  authors, not by NeuroSem.
- **[Dev probe]**: a development check in `docs/pilot/dt_probe.md`. It guided settings but
  is not a study result.
- **[Synthetic]**: a tool applied to an artificial trace. It shows how the tool behaves,
  not how a model behaves.
- **[Arithmetic]**: a calculation on the numbers above.
- **[Expectation]**: what we think will happen. It is not verified.
- **[Toy]**: invented numbers used only to explain a method.

No section contains a NeuroSem study result, because none exists yet.

## 1. What a conductance-based neuron model represents

**Analogy.** Picture a bathtub. The water level is the voltage. Taps pour water in and
drains let it out. Each drain opens or closes depending on the water level. The shape of
the tub decides how fast the level moves.

**The idea, step by step.**

- A neuron's membrane stores electric charge. That charge sets the voltage.
- Ion channels are the taps and drains. Each one lets a current flow.
- Each current depends on two things: how open the channel is, and how far the voltage is
  from the channel's reversal potential.
- Channels open and close as the voltage changes. That feedback makes spikes.
- The model is a set of equations that follows all of this over time.

**Precise definition.** A conductance-based model describes membrane voltage V with
C dV/dt = -(sum of channel currents) + injected current. Each channel current is
g x (gating variables) x (V - E_rev). Gating variables follow their own voltage-dependent
equations.

**Worked example: the RS cell.**

- **[File fact]** `RS.cell.nml` describes one soma, 96 um across. It has four channels:
  - Leak: 0.1 mS/cm2, reversal -70 mV.
  - Kd (fast potassium): 5.0 mS/cm2, reversal -100 mV.
  - IM (slow potassium): 0.07 mS/cm2, reversal -100 mV.
  - Na (sodium): 50 mS/cm2, reversal +50 mV.
- **[File fact]** Capacitance is 1.0 uF/cm2, and the start voltage is -70 mV.
- **[Arithmetic]** A zero-length segment counts as a sphere, so the area is
  pi x 96^2 um2 = 28,953 um2 = 2.895e-4 cm2. The NeuroML HH example confirms this rule: its
  17.84 um sphere is labelled 1000 um2.
- **[Arithmetic]** Total leak conductance = 0.1 mS/cm2 x 2.895e-4 cm2 = 28.95 nS.
- **[Arithmetic]** Total capacitance = 1 uF/cm2 x 2.895e-4 cm2 = 289.5 pF.
- **[Arithmetic]** Time constant from the leak alone = 289.5 pF / 28.95 nS = 10 ms. This
  ignores the other channels, which are also partly open at rest.

**Self-check.**

1. In the bathtub picture, what plays the role of the reversal potential?
2. Why does the RS cell's IM channel make it adapt, while the Na channel makes it spike?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. The water level at which a particular tap or drain stops moving water. At the
   reversal potential a channel's current is zero.
2. Na opens within a fraction of a millisecond and pushes the voltage up, which is the
   spike. IM opens slowly over many spikes and pulls the voltage down, so firing slows
   (**[Expectation]** for RS, suggested by its file notes and reference spike times).

</details>

## 2. What a current stimulus and a voltage trace are

**Analogy.** A stimulus is a pattern of pushing on a swing. The voltage trace is a video of
where the swing is at each moment.

**The idea, step by step.**

- We choose a current and inject it into the model cell.
- The current can be a step (on, then off), a ramp (slowly rising) or a short pulse.
- The simulator computes the voltage at every time step.
- The list of (time, voltage) pairs is the voltage trace.
- We never look only at "did it spike". We measure the trace.

**Precise definition.** A current-clamp stimulus is a function I(t) in nanoamps, applied
to the cell. The voltage trace is V(t) in millivolts, sampled on a uniform time grid.

**Worked example: the RS shipped test.**

- **[File fact]** `RS.net.nml` has a `pulseGenerator` with delay 0.3 s, duration 0.4 s and
  amplitude 7.5E-10 A. That is a 0.75 nA step from 300 to 700 ms.
- **[File fact]** `LEMS_RS.xml` runs 1000 ms at step 0.001 ms, which is 1,000,000 steps.
- **[File fact]** The output file stores seconds and volts. NeuroSem converts them to ms
  and mV (`load_dat`).
- **[Arithmetic]** With a leak-only resistance of 1 / 28.95 nS = 34.5 megaohms, a 0.75 nA
  step would push the voltage up by about 0.75 x 34.5 = 26 mV. That would reach about
  -44 mV. This is rough: the real cell has more open channels and spikes instead.

**Self-check.**

1. What three numbers describe a `pulseGenerator`?
2. Why must NeuroSem convert jLEMS output from seconds and volts before using eFEL?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Delay (when it starts), duration (how long) and amplitude (how strong).
2. eFEL assumes milliseconds and millivolts. With volts, a -20 mV threshold is never
   crossed, so it silently finds no spikes.

</details>

## 3. What spike count, latency, rheobase, adaptation and rebound mean

These are five small ideas, taken one at a time.

**3a. Spike count.**

- *Analogy:* counting how many times a kettle whistles in one minute.
- *Definition:* the number of action potentials inside the analysis window.
- *Example:* **[Upstream data]** RS reference spikes are at 320.554, 348.522, 387.944,
  456.69 and 592.105 ms. That is 5 spikes.

**3b. First-spike latency.**

- *Analogy:* how long after you press the doorbell someone answers.
- *Definition:* the time from stimulus onset to the first spike. eFEL measures to the spike
  peak.
- *Example:* **[Arithmetic]** 320.554 - 300 = 20.55 ms. **[Synthetic]** eFEL on an
  artificial trace with spikes at those times gave 20.55 ms.

**3c. Rheobase.**

- *Analogy:* the lightest push that makes a door swing all the way open.
- *Definition:* the smallest 500 ms step current that makes at least one spike.
- *How NeuroSem finds it:* try 12 amplitudes from 0 to 0.5 nA. Find the first one that
  spikes. Zoom into the gap below it three more times.
- *Example:* **[Arithmetic]** The first grid spacing is 0.5 / 11 = 0.045 nA. After three
  zooms of 11x the gap is 0.045 / 1331 = 0.000034 nA.

**3d. Adaptation.**

- *Analogy:* a runner who starts fast and slows down.
- *Definition:* intervals between spikes lengthen during a constant stimulus. The
  adaptation index summarises this. It is about 0 for steady firing and positive for
  slowing.
- *Example:* **[Arithmetic]** The RS reference intervals are 27.97, 39.42, 68.75 and
  135.42 ms. They keep growing. **[Synthetic]** eFEL's `adaptation_index2` on those spike
  times = 0.30.

**3e. Rebound.**

- *Analogy:* hold a spring down, let go, and it jumps up past where it started.
- *Definition:* spikes or depolarisation right after a hyperpolarising (negative) current
  stops.
- *Example:* **[File fact]** LTS contains the T-type calcium channel IT, a known rebound
  mechanism. **[Expectation]** LTS should fire after release in `P08_rebound`. No upstream
  test checks this.

**Self-check.**

1. A cell fires 3 spikes at 2 x rheobase and 3 spikes at 1.5 x rheobase. Which features
   could still differ?
2. Why does NeuroSem scale protocols to rheobase instead of using the same nA for every
   model?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Latency, interspike intervals, adaptation index, spike amplitude and width. The same
   count can hide different timing.
2. The same current can be too weak for one model and far too strong for another. Scaling
   to rheobase makes "2 x rheobase" mean a comparable test everywhere.

</details>

## 4. Schema validity, execution, numerical convergence, tested behaviour preservation, and biological validity

**Analogy.** Think of a recipe.

- *Schema validity:* the recipe is written in proper recipe format.
- *Execution:* you can actually cook it without the oven exploding.
- *Numerical convergence:* using a finer kitchen scale does not change the dish.
- *Tested behaviour preservation:* the edited recipe tastes the same as the original in
  the tastings you ran.
- *Biological validity:* the dish tastes like the real traditional dish.

**The idea, step by step.**

- These are five different questions. Passing one does not answer the next.
- NeuroSem checks the first four in order.
- NeuroSem never checks biological validity. It compares an edited model with its own
  original.

**Precise definitions.**

1. **Schema validity.** `jnml -validate` accepts the files.
2. **Execution.** jLEMS builds and runs the model and writes output.
3. **Numerical convergence.** Features are stable when the time step is halved.
4. **Tested behaviour preservation.** All tested (protocol, feature) differences stay
   within tolerance.
5. **Biological validity.** Agreement with real neurons (outside NeuroSem).

**Worked example: LTS shows how the layers can disagree.**

- **[File fact / M0 evidence]** On schema validity, the tools disagree about LTS.
  libNeuroML rejects it, because its custom gate type `IT_s_gate` is not in the schema
  list. jNeuroML accepts it.
- **[M0 evidence]** On execution, the engine matters. PyLEMS crashed on LTS
  (ZeroDivisionError), while jNeuroML runs it.
- **[Dev probe]** On convergence, the LTS third spike was at 540.53 ms with step 0.005 ms
  and 522.30 ms with step 0.001 ms. The first spike barely moved (431.51 versus 431.44 ms).
- **[Upstream data]** On tested preservation, the RS upstream OMV test compares 5 spike
  times with relative tolerance 0.00218. That is one test, one stimulus.

**Self-check.**

1. A mutant passes `jnml -validate` and runs, and its canonical spike times match. Which
   layers has it passed, and which has it not been tested on?
2. Why is "jNeuroML version" part of the definition of structural validity in NeuroSem?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. It passed schema validity, execution and one tested-behaviour check (canonical). It has
   not been tested on other stimuli, on convergence of its own differences, or on biology.
2. Validators disagree (LTS). "Valid" only means "valid according to this tool and
   version", so the version must be reported.

</details>

## 5. What a mutation and a valid transformation are

**Analogy.**

- A mutation is a deliberate typo in a recipe, such as "2 cups salt" instead of "2 tsp",
  made on purpose to test whether the tasters notice.
- A valid transformation is rewriting "500 g" as "0.5 kg". The page looks different, but
  the dish must not change.

**The idea, step by step.**

- A mutation changes exactly one thing, and we record exactly what.
- A valid transformation changes the representation, not the meaning.
- If the tests flag a mutation, good. If they flag a valid transformation, that is a false
  alarm.

**Precise definitions.**

- **Mutant.** A model produced by exactly one recorded mutation operator.
- **Valid transformation.** A representational change intended to preserve tested
  behaviour. Detection on it is a false positive.

**Worked example.**

- **[File fact]** RS IM density is `0.07 mS_per_cm2`.
- *Mutation (`scale_conductance`, factor 0.5):* 0.07 becomes `0.035 mS_per_cm2`. That is a
  real change to the model.
- *Valid transformation (`unit_conversion`):* **[Arithmetic]** 1 mS/cm2 = 10 S/m2, so
  0.07 mS_per_cm2 becomes `0.7 S_per_m2`. The value is the same, only written differently.
- **[File fact]** LTS already writes IM as `3e-5 S_per_cm2`, which equals 0.03 mS/cm2.
  Different files use different units, so this transformation happens in real life.

**Self-check.**

1. Is changing `erev="-70.0 mV"` to `erev="-70 mV"` a mutation or a valid transformation?
2. Why must a mutant contain only one change?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. A valid transformation (`numeric_literal_format`): same number, different spelling.
2. So that any detected difference can be blamed on that one change. With two changes we
   could not tell which one caused the drift.

</details>

## 6. Why equivalent mutants must not count as missed errors

**Analogy.** You hide a "mistake" in an exam answer key, but it is in a question nobody
was asked. The graders did not "miss" it, because there was nothing wrong for them to see.

**The idea, step by step.**

- Some mutations do not change behaviour at all, at least not for the stimuli we test.
- If no test detects them, no test failed.
- Counting them as misses would make every method look worse for no reason.
- So equivalent mutants (class 4) are left out of the detection-rate denominator.
- They are still reported, so nothing is hidden.

**Precise definition.** Class 4: the mutant executes, and no (protocol, feature) difference
exceeds tolerance reproducibly at h and h/2. It is "equivalent within the tested domain",
never "proven equivalent".

**Worked example.**

- **[File fact]** RS has one segment. Its groups `Soma`, `all` and `soma_group` all contain
  that same segment.
- **[Arithmetic / Expectation]** Moving a channel from group `all` to group `Soma` changes
  the file but applies the channel to the same membrane. The behaviour should be identical.
- **[Arithmetic / Expectation]** Shifting RS start voltage from -70 to -65 mV: every probe
  waits 300 ms before stimulating, which is about 30 leak time constants of 10 ms.
  The start difference fades by a factor of e^-30 (effectively zero). A likely equivalent
  mutant.

**Self-check.**

1. Why is "no difference found" not the same as "proven equivalent"?
2. What would happen to the canonical protocol's detection rate if equivalent mutants
   were counted as misses?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. We only tested a finite set of stimuli. A stimulus we never tried could still reveal a
   difference.
2. It would drop, and so would every other strategy's, because of changes that do not
   change anything. That makes the comparison unfair and uninformative.

</details>

## 7. What silent semantic drift means operationally

**Analogy.** A car passes its yearly safety check (lights, brakes on a flat road), but its
brakes fail on steep hills. The check was real, but it only tested one situation.

**The idea, step by step.** A mutant shows silent drift when all of these are true:

1. It passes `jnml -validate`.
2. It runs without crashing and stays numerically stable.
3. The canonical (shipped) test shows no detection at h.
4. Some other protocol shows a detection, and the change is reproducible: some detection
   holds at both h and h/2.

**Precise definition.** Silent semantic drift is mutant class 6: non-equivalent and not
detected by the canonical protocol at h.

**Worked example: walking one mutant through the cascade.** **[Toy]** Imagine RS with IM
density halved.

- Validate: IM is still a valid number, so it passes.
- Run: the model runs, so it is executable.
- Canonical (0.75 nA, 300-700 ms): suppose spike count stays 5, and every timing change is
  below its tolerance. No canonical detection.
- `P05_long_step` (2 s): suppose the last interspike interval changes by more than its
  tolerance, at both h and h/2.
- Result: class 6, silent drift. This is a toy walk-through, not a result. **[Expectation]**
  A slow current like IM has more time to matter in a 2 s step.

**Self-check.**

1. A mutant is detected by the canonical test and by P05. Is it silent drift?
2. A mutant is detected only by P05, but only at h, not at h/2. What class is it likely to
   be?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. It is non-equivalent (class 5), because canonical caught it.
2. Probably class 4 (equivalent within the tested domain), unless another detection
   reproduces at both steps. A difference that disappears when the step is halved looks
   like solver error, not a real change.

</details>

## 8. What a perturbation fingerprint measures

**Analogy.** A doctor's check-up does not measure only your pulse. It records pulse, blood
pressure, reflexes, temperature and more. That whole panel is your fingerprint.

**The idea, step by step.**

- Run the model under several stimuli (protocols).
- From each response, measure several features.
- Collect every (protocol, feature) value into one table.
- Compare the edited model's table with the original's, entry by entry.

**Precise definition.** F(m) = { f(m, p) : p in P, f in E }, the set of feature values over
all protocols P and features E.

**Worked example.**

- **[File fact]** The current templates list P01 4, P02 4, P03 1, P04 11, P05 11, P06 4,
  P07 5, P08 4, P09 5 and P10 4 features, which is 53 entries.
- The canonical test adds 11 more.
- **[Arithmetic]** 53 + 11 = **64 entries per model, per time step**.
- Some entries are "not applicable". For example, AP width in a protocol where the cell
  stays silent. Those are recorded as such, never as zero.

**Self-check.**

1. Why is it important that a missing feature is recorded as "undefined" and not as 0?
2. How many fingerprint entries does `P04_step_2x` contribute, and what kind are they?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. A zero is a real value. If the original has a spike width of 1.2 ms and the mutant has
   none, storing 0 invents a number. Recording "undefined" lets NeuroSem count the
   definedness change honestly.
2. 11, all spiking features: count, frequency, latency, AP amplitude, AP half-width, AHP
   depth, first ISI, last ISI, adaptation index, burst count, firing regime.

</details>

## 9. Why multiple protocols can reveal changes hidden by one protocol

**Analogy.** Testing a bridge only with one small car proves little about trucks, wind or
ice. Different loads reveal different weaknesses.

**The idea, step by step.**

- Each channel matters most in certain conditions.
- A slow current needs a long stimulus to show its effect.
- A current that wakes up after hyperpolarisation needs a negative stimulus first.
- One stimulus only visits some of these conditions.
- More, and different, stimuli visit more conditions.

**Precise definition.** Two models can agree on every feature under protocol p and still
differ under protocol q. The map from parameters to behaviour depends on the stimulus.

**Worked example.**

- **[File fact]** The RS and LTS shipped tests each use one **positive** step: 0.75 nA for
  400 ms, and 0.15 nA for 400 ms. Neither ever hyperpolarises the cell.
- **[File fact]** The LTS IT channel's inactivation gate `u` is slow (a custom time course
  with a temperature factor).
- **[Expectation]** A change to IT may barely show in the positive canonical step but
  clearly change `P08_rebound`, which releases the cell from -2 x rheobase.
- **[Expectation]** A change to RS's slow IM current may be clearer in `P05_long_step`
  (2000 ms) than in the 400 ms canonical step.

**Self-check.**

1. Which protocol would you expect to reveal a change in a current that is only active
   below rest?
2. Give one reason a single canonical test might miss a change in a slow current.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. A hyperpolarising one: `P07_hyperpolarizing_step` or `P08_rebound`.
2. The canonical step may end before the slow current has had time to build up, so the
   features in its window barely move.

</details>

## 10. How greedy protocol selection works

**Analogy.** You want to catch as many fish as possible with only 3 nets. You first place
the net that catches the most fish. Then you place the net that catches the most fish
*not already caught*. Then the next.

**The idea, step by step.**

1. Make a table: rows are mutants, columns are protocols, and a tick means "detects".
2. Pick the protocol with the most ticks.
3. Cross out the mutants it detects.
4. Pick the protocol with the most ticks among the remaining mutants.
5. Repeat until you have k protocols.
6. On a tie, pick the cheaper protocol, then the lower protocol id.

**Precise definition.** Greedy maximum coverage on the discovery detection matrix, with
budget k, deterministic ties by cost then id. It is simple and not always optimal. For
maximum coverage, greedy is known to reach at least about 63 % (1 - 1/e) of the best
possible coverage.

**Worked example.** **[Toy]** The ticks are invented. The costs are the real cell-steps at
h (**[Arithmetic]**).

| mutant | P04 (200,000) | P05 (500,000) | P08 (220,000) | P09 (100,000) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | x | x | | |
| M2 | | x | | |
| M3 | | | x | |
| M4 | x | | | x |
| M5 | | x | x | |
| M6 | | | x | x |

- **Step 1.** Ticks: P04 2, P05 3, P08 3, P09 2. P05 and P08 tie at 3, and P08 is cheaper
  (220,000 versus 500,000), so pick **P08**. It covers M3, M5 and M6.
- **Step 2.** Remaining M1, M2, M4. Ticks: P04 2 (M1, M4), P05 2 (M1, M2), P09 1 (M4).
  P04 and P05 tie, and P04 is cheaper, so pick **P04**. Covered so far: M1, M3, M4, M5, M6.
- **Step 3.** Remaining M2. Only P05 ticks it, so pick **P05**. All 6 are covered.
- **Coverage curve:** 3/6, then 5/6, then 6/6.

**Self-check.**

1. Why does step 2 count only *remaining* mutants?
2. Why must the selection be done only on discovery mutants?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Mutants already caught add no value. We want the protocol that adds the most new
   detections.
2. If held-out mutants influenced the choice, testing on them would be like marking your
   own exam with the answer key you studied from.

</details>

## 11. Why held-out models and mutation families matter

**Analogy.** A student who practises on last year's exam paper and then sits the *same*
paper has shown memory, not understanding. A new paper, and ideally a new kind of
question, tests real learning.

**The idea, step by step.**

- We choose protocols using some models and some mutation types: the discovery set.
- A choice can fit quirks of those models.
- So we test the frozen choice on models it never saw.
- And on at least one whole kind of mutation it never saw.
- If it still works, the battery generalises. If not, we learned it only fitted the
  discovery set.

**Precise definition.**

- Held-out models and a held-out mutation family must not influence protocol selection or
  tolerances.
- Split files are hashed, selection code cannot read held-out files, and every held-out
  access is logged.

**Worked example.**

- **[File fact / M0 evidence]** The four Pospischil cells (RS, LTS, FS, IB) share
  byte-identical `Na`, `Kd` and `Leak` channel files. RS, LTS and IB also share `IM`, and
  the same SHA-256 hashes are recorded for every cell.
- **[Arithmetic reasoning]** If RS were in discovery and FS in held-out, FS would share
  most of its kinetics with a model the selection already saw. A good held-out result would
  overstate generalisation.

**Self-check.**

1. Why is a held-out *mutation family* a harder test than randomly holding out some
   mutants?
2. Why is it a problem to look at held-out failures and then redesign the protocols?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Random mutants come from the same kinds of fault the battery was tuned on. A whole
   unseen family tests faults of a new kind.
2. The held-out set would then have influenced the design, so it is no longer an
   independent test.

</details>

## 12. How tolerances are calibrated

**Analogy.** If your bathroom scale jumps by 0.3 kg when you step on it twice, a 0.2 kg
"weight change" means nothing. You first measure how noisy the scale is. Then you only
trust changes bigger than that noise.

**The idea, step by step.**

- Even the unedited model gives slightly different numbers when the time step changes.
- That difference is the solver's own "noise".
- For each model, protocol and feature, NeuroSem runs the original at h and at h/2.
- The tolerance is the largest of three things:
  1. a fixed floor (the smallest change that matters scientifically);
  2. a small fraction of the value itself;
  3. three times the difference between h and h/2.
- A mutant is only "detected" if its difference is bigger than this tolerance.
- The rules are frozen before held-out testing.

**Precise definition.**
tau(m, p, f) = max(abs_floor_f, rel_f x |f_h|, c x |f_h - f_h/2|), with c = 3
(provisional, `configs/tolerances.yaml`).

**Worked example.** These use shipped-harness spike times, with no NeuroSem features yet.
Spike times come from **[Dev probe]**. The floors for latency and ISI (`abs_floor`
0.5 ms, `rel` 0.02) are **[File fact]**. All calculations are **[Arithmetic]**.

*A stable measurement: RS first spike latency (onset 300 ms).*

- h = 0.005 ms: 320.63 - 300 = 20.63 ms. h/2: 320.59 - 300 = 20.59 ms.
- Floor 0.5. Fraction 0.02 x 20.63 = 0.41. Refinement 3 x 0.04 = 0.12.
- Tolerance = **0.5 ms** (the floor wins).

*A fragile measurement: LTS last interval.*

- h: 738.24 - 540.53 = 197.71 ms. h/2: 730.07 - 529.56 = 200.51 ms.
- Floor 0.5. Fraction 0.02 x 197.71 = 3.95. Refinement 3 x 2.80 = 8.40.
- Tolerance = **8.40 ms** (the refinement term wins).

The lesson: a fragile feature gets a wide tolerance, so it can only detect big changes.

**Self-check.**

1. Why is the refinement term multiplied by 3 instead of used as it is?
2. Why must tolerances never be changed after seeing held-out results?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. The h versus h/2 difference is only an estimate of solver error. A safety factor keeps
   ordinary solver error from being called drift.
2. Changing them after seeing results lets the results choose the rules, so the test is no
   longer honest.

</details>

## 13. What Claude built versus what Claude was experimentally evaluated on

**Analogy.** A lab technician who builds the microscope is different from a volunteer whose
cells go under the microscope. Mixing the two roles would ruin the experiment.

**The idea, step by step.**

- **Role 1, builder.** Claude Code helps write code, tests and documents. Neel reviews
  everything and makes the scientific decisions.
- **Role 2, test subject.** Later, in a separate optional experiment, fresh isolated
  Claude Code sessions get modelling tasks. Their edits are scored by hidden, frozen tests.
- The two roles never mix. The subject never sees the hidden tests that the builder
  helped create.

**Precise definition.**

- Development assistance is logged in `AI_USE_LOG.md`.
- The agent case study (Milestone 9) uses frozen NeuroSem, public tests only, hidden
  protocols and answer keys, clean commits, fresh sessions, fixed permissions and full
  transcripts.

**Worked example: status on 2026-09-13.**

- **[File fact]** `AI_USE_LOG.md` Entry 001 records Claude Code (Claude Opus 5) doing the
  Milestone 0 audit.
- Later software and these documents were also AI-assisted and still need log entries
  (`docs/AI_DISCLOSURE.md`).
- **[File fact]** No agent-as-subject trial exists.
- **[Expectation]** Any future claim is limited to "the evaluated Claude Code
  configuration", never to coding agents in general.

**Self-check.**

1. Why must the agent in the case study not see the selected perturbation protocols?
2. Which role has Claude played so far?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. If it saw the hidden tests, it could fix edits to pass them. We would then measure test
   leakage, not whether its edits preserve behaviour.
2. Only role 1, the development assistant.

</details>

## 14. Limits of every final claim

**Analogy.** A weather forecast that was right for your town all summer does not prove it
works in winter, or in another country. A good claim says exactly where it was tested.

**The idea, step by step.** Every future claim needs its limit attached. The limits
already known:

- **Finite tests.** "No detection" means "no difference in these protocols". It is not
  proof of equivalence.
- **Tested domain.** Equivalent means "within the tested stimuli", nothing more.
- **Chosen faults.** Detection rates describe *our* mutation operators, not how often real
  edits go wrong.
- **Few models.** 4-6 held-out models planned, 2 included so far. One odd model can swing
  the result.
- **Correlated models.** The four Pospischil cells share channel files. They are not
  independent evidence.
- **One held-out family.** Success on one unseen fault type does not cover all fault types.
- **One simulator.** jNeuroML with forward Euler. Another simulator may differ.
- **Tolerances.** Different tolerance choices can change conclusions, so sensitivity
  results must be shown.
- **No biology.** NeuroSem compares a model with itself. It says nothing about real
  neurons.
- **AI case study.** Only the evaluated Claude Code configuration, tasks and date.

**Precise definition.** A claim is supported only for the models, operators, protocols,
tolerances, simulator and configuration actually tested. The statistics must account for
clustering, and the per-model results must be shown.

**Worked example.** **[Toy]** Compare two sentences.

- *Too strong:* "NeuroSem proves edited neuron models keep their behaviour."
- *Properly limited:* "On [K] held-out single-compartment NeuroML models simulated with
  jNeuroML, a 4-protocol battery selected on discovery models detected [x] of [N]
  admissible single-fault mutants, versus [y] of [N] for the shipped canonical test (95 %
  cluster-bootstrap interval [..]). Detection is relative to the tested stimuli and frozen
  tolerances."

**Self-check.**

1. Rewrite "NeuroSem catches silent bugs in neuron models" with its limits.
2. Why can the study never claim that a transformation is *proven* safe?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. For example: "In the evaluated models and mutation operators, NeuroSem's selected
   battery detected some mutants that passed the canonical test, within the tested stimuli
   and tolerances."
2. Only a finite set of stimuli was tested. An untested stimulus could still reveal a
   difference. A certificate is evidence, not proof.
